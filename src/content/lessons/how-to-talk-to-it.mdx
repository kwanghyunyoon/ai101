The same tool gives a weak answer or a strong one depending on how you ask. This
Lesson is the part that pays off every time you use it: a few habits that turn a
vague reply into one you can actually use.

## Give it context

The model cannot see your situation. It only has the words in your prompt. So
tell it what a person helping you would need to know:

- **Who you are:** "I'm a parent," "I run a small bakery," "I'm new to this job."
- **The purpose:** what the text is for and what you want it to achieve.
- **The audience:** who will read it — a landlord, a child, a hiring manager.

A prompt with context does not need to be long. It needs to answer "for whom,
and why."

## State the form you want

Tell the model the shape of the answer, or it will pick one for you:

- **Length:** "three sentences," "one paragraph," "a short list."
- **Format:** a list, a table, an email, bullet points.
- **Tone:** warm, plain, formal, direct.

If you do not say, you often get five paragraphs when you wanted three lines.

## Show an example

If you have a sample of what you want, paste it in. Write "Here is the style I'm
going for:" and add a short example. One example does more than a paragraph of
description. The model is good at matching a pattern you hand it.

## Treat the first answer as a draft

The first reply is a starting point. Read it, then say what to change, in the
same chat:

- "Make it shorter."
- "Drop the last paragraph."
- "Say it more simply — my reader is 70 and not technical."

The model keeps the earlier context, so each follow-up builds on it. This back
and forth is normal and it is where most of the quality comes from.

## Break a big ask into steps

For anything with several parts — a trip, a budget, a long document — do not ask
for all of it at once. Ask for an outline first. Then work through one piece per
message. Small answers are easier to check, and you catch a wrong turn before
the model builds on it.

## "Act as" and "write for"

You can point the model at a role or a reader:

- "Act as a careful proofreader and mark what is unclear."
- "Explain this like you are talking to a 10-year-old."
- "Write this for someone who has never used a computer."

This is a shortcut for setting tone and level without spelling out every rule.

## Before and after: the same task, two prompts

The task: email your building manager about a leaking kitchen tap.

**Lazy prompt:**

> Write an email about my sink.

You get a generic email. It invents details — how long it has leaked, how bad it
is, a unit number — because you did not give any. You have to rewrite most of it.

**Better prompt:**

> Write a short, polite email to my building manager. I'm in apartment 4B. The
> kitchen tap has dripped for three days and it's getting worse. Ask them to
> send someone this week. Keep it to four sentences.

Now the email has your real details, the right length, a clear ask, and a tone
you can send as is. Same tool, same task — the difference is entirely in the
prompt.

## Try it: prompt it badly, then improve it twice

Pick a real task — say, a message to cancel a standing appointment.

1. **Round one:** paste "Write a message canceling my appointment." See how much
   it has to guess.
2. **Round two:** add context and form. Who is it to, how formal, how long, and
   do you want a new time offered?
3. **Round three:** reply to its answer with one fix — shorter, warmer, or add a
   specific reason.

<TryIt
  prompt="Write a message canceling my appointment."
  lookFor="A generic cancellation with placeholders or invented specifics. This is your baseline — the next two rounds should visibly beat it."
/>

Each round should get closer to something you would actually send. That gap,
closing, is prompting.
