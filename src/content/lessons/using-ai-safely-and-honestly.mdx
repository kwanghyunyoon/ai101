These tools are useful, but how you use them matters. This Lesson covers a few
habits. Keep private things out of the chat. Check facts before you trust them.
Say when you used AI. Watch for bias, opt out of training if you want, and treat
the tool as a tool, not an authority.

## Do not paste in secrets or other people's private data

Whatever you type into a chatbot leaves your device and goes to a company's
servers. On a free plan, it may also be stored and used to improve the model,
and staff may review some chats.

So keep these out of the box:

- Passwords, bank or card numbers, and one-time codes.
- Your full ID numbers — passport, national ID, social security.
- Anything private about someone else: a friend's health, a coworker's contract,
  a client's records.
- Work documents your employer treats as confidential.

You can still get help. Describe the situation in general words. Swap real names
for "my manager" or "the client." The tool does not need the real details to fix
your tone or structure.

## Verify anything factual before you rely on it

An earlier Lesson showed that these tools can state a wrong fact in a confident
voice. That does not stop mattering when the answer sounds careful.

Some facts need a real source before you act on them. A date, a price, a legal
rule, a medical dose, a citation. Confirm each one against a search tool that
links to pages, or a person whose job it is to know.

## Say when you used AI, where it matters

For a private text or your own to-do list, how you wrote it is your business. It
is different when someone is judging the work as yours:

- **School:** many schools now have clear rules. Some allow AI help if you
  disclose it; some do not allow it at all. Ask before you use it.
- **Work:** your employer may have a policy on AI-written material, especially
  anything sent to customers or the public.
- **Publishing:** if you put something out under your name — an article, a
  review, a photo — readers are trusting it is yours. Disclose AI use when it
  would change how they read it.

The simple test: if someone would feel misled to learn AI helped, say so up
front.

## Its answers carry the bias of its training text

The model learned from a huge amount of human writing, and that writing is not
evenly spread. Some places, languages, and viewpoints are far more represented
than others.

So its "normal" answer leans toward whatever was most common in that text. Ask it
for a picture of a "doctor" or a list of "great novels" and the lean shows. It is
not neutral, and it does not know that. Treat its default as one common view, not
the only one.

## How to keep your chats out of training

Most major tools now have a setting to keep your conversations out of model
training. The exact path changes, so check the app. As of late 2026:

- **ChatGPT:** in Settings, open Data Controls and turn off the option about
  improving the model for everyone.
- **Gemini:** in Settings, turn off "Gemini Apps Activity" (also at
  myactivity.google.com).
- **Claude:** in Settings, open Privacy and turn off the option about helping
  improve Claude.
- **Copilot (Microsoft):** in Settings, look under Privacy for a model-training
  toggle and turn it off.
- **Grok:** in Settings, look for a data-sharing option about training and turn
  it off.

Turning chat history off usually drops it from training too. The switch helps,
but it is a backstop. The main habit is still to keep sensitive things out of
the chat.

## It is a tool, not an authority

A chatbot has no job title, no license, and no accountability. It cannot be your
doctor, lawyer, accountant, or therapist. It can help you understand a topic,
draft a question for a real professional, or organize what you already know.

For decisions about your health, money, legal standing, or safety, a person or an
official source has to be the one you rely on.
